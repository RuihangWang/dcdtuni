# Three Key Writing Concepts: Word Spikes, Word Meandering, Fluidity

## 1. Word Spikes

**Definition:** An unnecessarily complex or rare word used where a simpler, more common word would suffice. The "spike" metaphor refers to a sudden jump in reading difficulty that forces the reader to pause, decode the word, and re-engage — breaking the reading flow.

**Examples from scientific writing:**
- "leverage" instead of "use"
- "corroborate" instead of "confirm"
- "extraordinarily" instead of "very" or just removing the intensifier
- "utilize" instead of "use"
- "elucidate" instead of "explain"
- "ameliorate" instead of "improve"
- "endeavour" instead of "try"

**Why it matters:** Scientific papers are read by international audiences, many of whom are non-native English speakers. Word spikes create unnecessary cognitive load without adding precision. The reader's working memory is diverted from understanding the IDEA to decoding the WORD.

**Lebrun's perspective:** Writing should be invisible — the reader should focus on the science, not the vocabulary. Every word that makes the reader pause is a "reading accident."

## 2. Word Meandering (Delayed Main Verb)

**Definition:** A sentence pattern where the subject is separated from its main verb by a long chain of modifiers, subordinate clauses, parenthetical insertions, or appositives. The reader must hold the subject in working memory while navigating through intervening material before reaching the verb that tells them what the subject DOES.

**Structure pattern:**
```
Subject ... [long modifier] ... [parenthetical] ... [relative clause] ... main verb
```

**Why it matters:** Human working memory is limited (Miller's 7±2 chunks). When a reader encounters the subject, they immediately seek the verb to form a proposition. If the verb is delayed by 15-20 words of intervening material, the reader either:
1. Loses the subject and must re-read
2. Constructs a wrong prediction and must re-parse
Both are "reading accidents" that break comprehension flow.

**Fix strategies:**
- Move the main verb closer to the subject (within 7-10 words)
- Split the sentence into two
- Move long modifiers to before the subject or after the verb
- Convert parenthetical content into a separate sentence

**Related literature:**
- Creating Logical Flow When Writing Scientific Articles (PMC, 2021): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8524230/

## 3. Fluidity

**Definition:** The smooth, logical progression of ideas from sentence to sentence and paragraph to paragraph. Good fluidity means each sentence naturally leads to the next, with "old information" appearing before "new information" (the given-new contract).

**Key principles:**
- **Given-New Contract:** Each sentence should begin with information the reader already knows (given) and end with new information. The new information of sentence N becomes the given information of sentence N+1.
- **Topic strings:** Consecutive sentences in a paragraph should maintain a consistent topic thread.
- **Logical connectors:** Transitions between ideas should be explicit when the connection isn't obvious, but invisible when the logic is self-evident.
- **Paragraph-level flow:** Each paragraph should have a clear topic sentence, and paragraphs should link logically.

**Fluidity breakers:**
- Abrupt topic shifts between consecutive sentences
- Parenthetical figure/table references that interrupt the sentence flow: "(Figure 1)" instead of "as illustrated in Figure 1"
- "Orphan" sections: a section heading immediately followed by a subsection heading with no introductory text
- Missing transitions between paragraphs

**Lebrun's tools:**
- Fluiditext web app: highlights potential text progression problems
- SWAN: assesses fluidity and cohesion as paper-quality attributes
